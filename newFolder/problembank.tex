\textbf{Icebreaker/Group Introduction}
\begin{exercise}
   Before class, assign each seat a role card. Students sit down and find their card — they don't choose their groups, which is realistic and worth acknowledging out loud.
Each group of four has one shared problem: a real-world scenario where something is changing, presented without any calculus notation. For example:

A coffee cup is cooling. At the moment you pour it, the temperature is dropping fast. Twenty minutes later, it's barely changing. How would you describe — precisely — how fast it's cooling at any single moment in time?

No formulas are given. The point isn't to solve it with calculus yet — it's to think about it.

The Roles
Each student gets a card that gives them one piece of the puzzle — and only one. The roles are:
The Skeptic — Your job is to question every assumption the group makes. Nothing gets accepted without justification. You ask: "How do we know that? What are we assuming here?"
The Visualizer — You must draw what's happening. No equations allowed from you — only sketches, graphs, and diagrams. You translate the group's ideas into pictures.
The Connector — You've been given one hint: "Think about what happens when you zoom in very closely on a curve." Your job is to introduce this idea at the right moment and help the group see how it connects to their discussion.
The Summarizer — Every five minutes, you pause the group and restate what's been agreed on so far in plain English. You also write the group's final answer.
The key design feature: no single role has enough to solve the problem alone. The Visualizer needs the Skeptic to sharpen the picture. The Connector's hint only makes sense once the group has wrestled with the question. The Summarizer can't summarize what hasn't been discussed.

The Activity Flow
Minutes 0-2: Individual think time. Everyone reads the scenario silently and jots down their own initial instinct. No talking yet. This ensures quieter students have something to contribute before the extroverts set the tone.
Minutes 2-15: Group discussion. The group works through the problem using their roles. The instructor circulates and says almost nothing — just listens and occasionally asks "Has the Summarizer caught us up lately?"
Minutes 15-20: Gallery share. Each group's Visualizer puts their sketch on the board (whiteboard, paper taped up, or shared screen). Groups do a quick silent gallery walk, looking at how other groups drew the same scenario differently.
Minutes 20-25: Debrief. The instructor brings everyone back and asks three questions:

"What did your group figure out that you couldn't have figured out alone?"
"Which role was hardest? Why?"
"Where did you get stuck — and what unstuck you?"

Only after the debrief does the instructor connect the scenario to the formal concept of the derivative — the limit definition, the tangent line, the idea of instantaneous rate of change. Now the formalism has something to attach to.\\
(Generated with Claude Sonnet 4.6, prompt: ``design an activity to be used as an icebreaker for an undergraduate calculus class to help students get used to working in groups and see why working in groups is important'')
\end{exercise}


\textbf{Function Review}
 \begin{exercise}
   Suppose that you have 400 feet of fencing, and you would like to build a rectangular animal pen against a barn.  Fencing is not required on the side along the barn.  
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Draw a sketch of the situation, and label your sketch. 
        \item Write a function, $A(x)$, that represents the area of the enclosed pen.  Your equation should have only one unknown.
        \item What is the domain of $A(x)$?
    \end{enumerate}
 \end{exercise}

 \textbf{Intermediate Value Theorem}
  \begin{exercise}
      \begin{enumerate}
         \item Let $h$ be a continuous function on $[-1,1]$ such that $f(-1)=-10$ and $f(1)=10$.  Does a value $c$ with $-1<c<1$ exist such that $h(c)=0$ (i.e. $c$ is a root or solution of $h$)?  Explain why or why not.
         \item Let $h$ be a continuous function on $[-1,1]$ such that $f(-1)=-10$ and $f(1)=10$.  Does a value $c$ with $-1<c<1$ exist such that $h(c)=11$?  Explain why or why not.
      \end{enumerate}
      Problems from Hartman, et al., \textit{APEX Calculus}.
  \end{exercise}

  \begin{exercise}
      Consider the function $f(x)=2x^3+3x^2+7$. For what values of $k$ does the Intermediate Value Theorem tell us that there is a $c$ in the interval $[0,1]$ such that $f(c)=k$? \\
      Problem from Wakefield, et al., \textit{Coordinated Calculus}.
  \end{exercise}

 \textbf{Infinite Limits and Indeterminate Forms}
   \begin{exercise}
      Find the limit, using one-sided limits if necessary: $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 2} \frac{x^2+6x-4}{x^2-4}$
   \end{exercise}

   \begin{exercise}
     $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 3}\frac{x^2-3x+2}{x-3}$\\
     %{\blue This limit DNE. We see that $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow3^+}f(x)=\infty$ while $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 3^-}f(x)=-\infty$.}
   \end{exercise}

   \begin{exercise}
      $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 2} \frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}+4}{x-4}$
    %{\blue $=\dfrac{\sqrt{4-2^2}+4}{2-4}=\dfrac{4}{-2}=-2$}
   \end{exercise}

   \begin{exercise}
      $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 3}\frac{x^2-2x-3}{x-3}$
 %   \item $\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow -\infty}\frac{x^2-3x+2}{x-3}$
   \end{exercise}

   \begin{exercise}
      $\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 9}\frac{\sqrt{r}}{(r-9)^4}$
   \end{exercise}

   \begin{exercise}
     $\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow \pi^-} \ln(\sin(x))$\\
   \end{exercise}

   \begin{exercise}
      $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 1}\frac{\sqrt{x-1}}{(x-1)^2}$
    %{\blue $= \displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 1}\frac{(x-1)^{\frac12}}{(x-1)^2}=\lim_{x\rightarrow 1} \frac{1}{(x-1)^{\frac32}} $\\
    %Numerator is always positive.\\
    %Denominator is only defined when $x>1$.  Thus the limit does not exist.}\\
   \end{exercise}


\textbf{Sandwich/Squeeze Theorem}
   \begin{exercise}
     If $2x\leq g(x)\leq x^4-x^2+2$ for all $x$, evaluate $\displaystyle \lim_{x\rightarrow 1} g(x)$
   \end{exercise}

\textbf{Trig Limits}
   \begin{exercise}
    $\displaystyle\lim_{\theta \rightarrow 0}\frac{\sin(3\theta)\sin(5\theta)}{3\theta^2}$\\
    %{\color{blue} $=\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 0}\bigg(\frac{\sin(3\theta)}{3\theta}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\sin(5\theta)}{\theta}\bigg)\frac{5}{5}=\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 0}5\bigg(\frac{\sin(3\theta)}{3\theta}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\sin(5\theta)}{5\theta}\bigg)=5(1)(1)=5$}
    %\item $\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0} \frac{\cos(x)-1}{\tan(x)}$
   \end{exercise}

\textbf{Piecewise Derivatives}
   \begin{exercise}
      Calculate $f'(2)$ or explain why it does not exist for each example below.
      \begin{enumerate}
         \item \[f(x)=\begin{cases}
         -2x+1 & x\leq 2\\
         -x^2+1 & x>2
      \end{cases}\]
      \item \[f(x)=\begin{cases}
         -4x+1 & x\leq 2\\
         -x^2+1 & x>2
      \end{cases}\]
    \end{enumerate}
   \end{exercise}
